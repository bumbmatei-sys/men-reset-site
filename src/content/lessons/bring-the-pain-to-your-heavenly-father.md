Many people who have been hurt find it hard to pray. Not because they do not believe, but because the only prayers they know sound too neat. "Thank You, Lord, for this day." Meanwhile inside there is anger, grief, fear, and questions they are not sure they are allowed to ask.

So they stop praying, or they say the neat words and leave the real stuff outside the door.

There is another way, and it is all over the Bible. It is called lament.

## Where we are in this course

In lesson 1 we saw that what happened to you is not who you are. In lesson 2 we made a plan for when your body reacts. Now we turn to your heart. Where do you put the pain? You bring it to your heavenly Father, as it is.

## What are the psalms of lament?

A lament is a prayer that tells God the truth about pain. The book of Psalms is full of them. Bible teachers count them differently, but a large share of the Psalms are laments, more than any other kind.

Listen to how David prays: "How long, O LORD? Will You forget me forever? How long will You hide Your face from me? How long shall I take counsel in my soul, Having sorrow in my heart daily?" (Psalm 13:1-2).

That is not a polite prayer. It is a man telling God that it feels like He has disappeared. And God put that prayer in the Bible, for us to use.

Laments are not a lack of faith. They are what faith sounds like when it hurts. You only complain to someone you still believe is listening.

## You do not have to tidy it up first

Some of us grew up in homes where showing pain was dangerous. Crying got you mocked. Anger got you hit. Asking questions got you ignored. So we learned to say we are fine.

Your heavenly Father is not that home. "Trust in Him at all times, you people; Pour out your heart before Him; God is a refuge for us" (Psalm 62:8). Pour out. Not trickle out the safe parts. Pour.

He already knows what is in there. "You number my wanderings; Put my tears into Your bottle; Are they not in Your book?" (Psalm 56:8). Not one of your tears is lost on Him. He keeps them.

If you are angry at God, you can tell Him that too. Job did. Jeremiah did. God did not strike them down. He stayed with them.

## Jesus prayed this way too

At the tomb of His friend Lazarus, knowing He was about to raise him, Jesus still wept (John 11:35). He did not skip the grief.

In the garden before His death, "He began to be troubled and deeply distressed" and said, "My soul is exceedingly sorrowful, even to death" (Mark 14:33-34). On the cross He prayed the first line of a lament psalm: "My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?" (Psalm 22:1).

"For we do not have a High Priest who cannot sympathize with our weaknesses" (Hebrews 4:15). When you lament, you are not doing something strange. You are praying the way Jesus prayed.

## How to write a lament psalm

Most laments have the same simple shape. You can use it for your own prayer. It does not have to be long or beautiful. A few lines for each part is enough.

| Part | What it means | Starting words |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Turn to God | Speak to Him directly, by name. | "Father..." |
| 2. Tell the truth | Say what happened and how it feels, as plainly as you can. | "This is what hurts..." "I feel..." "Why...?" "How long...?" |
| 3. Ask | Tell Him what you need. Be specific. | "Please help me..." "Please let me sleep." "Please do not let it happen to anyone else." |
| 4. Trust, even a little | Name one thing you still believe about Him, even if it is small. | "But You are still..." "I will still..." |

Here is a short example, the kind of thing a man might write:

*Father, I am so tired of this. I thought I had put it behind me, and today it came back again when he raised his voice at work. I felt eight years old. Why did nobody stop it? Why did You let it go on? I am angry, and I am ashamed that I am angry. Please let me sleep tonight. Please show me You were there. I do not feel much right now. But You said You are near to the brokenhearted, so I am staying here with You.*

That is a real prayer. It is not tidy. It does not pretend. And it still turns toward God at the end.

You do not need to write details of what happened to you. "When it happened" or "that house" is enough. God knows the rest.

## When the trust part is very small

Some days you will get to part 4 and have almost nothing. That is all right.

Psalm 88 is the darkest prayer in the Bible. It does not end with a happy turn. Its last line is, "Loved one and friend You have put far from me, And my acquaintances into darkness" (Psalm 88:18). And yet the whole psalm is addressed to God. The man kept talking to Him in the dark. That is trust, even when it does not feel like it.

If your prayers feel flat right now, the lesson [Why You Can't Feel It](/tracks/know-gods-love/why-you-cant-feel-it) may help. And if you find it hard to pray because you also feel guilty about your own failures, [How to Pray When You Keep Falling](/teachings/how-to-pray-when-you-keep-falling) covers that side.

## A note on safety

Writing a lament can stir up strong feelings. Go slowly. Set a timer for ten minutes. When it rings, stop, put the paper away, and do something ordinary, like a walk or a meal. If writing brings up memories that overwhelm you, that is a good thing to bring to a counselor rather than pushing through alone.

If you ever feel unsafe or have thoughts of ending your life, do not wait. Tell someone near you today and get help right away, any hour.
